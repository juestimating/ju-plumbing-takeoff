Plumbing takeoff software vs service comes down to two questions: who has the hours this week, and who answers for the numbers. Software suits a team with a steady bid flow and an estimator who has time to measure, check and revise every set. A service suits bid pile-ups, unfamiliar building types and contractors who would rather price than measure. AI takeoff tools can speed up counting, but a person who reads plumbing drawings still has to verify the result.

## Takeoff software, AI tools and services: how each one works

- **Takeoff software** is a measuring tool you operate: you calibrate each sheet, mark pipe runs by size and material, count symbols and export quantities. Bluebeam Revu, PlanSwift and On-Screen Takeoff are the three we work in. The software does the arithmetic, and the judgment is yours.
- **AI takeoff tools** try to read the drawings for you: they detect and count symbols, trace some linework and read schedules, then hand you a list to review. Results vary by product and by drawing set.
- **A takeoff service** puts people on the measuring. You send the plans and get a finished file, ideally with a sheet reference on every line and a note of what was assumed.

A paper set and a scale still work on a small job; the guide on [how to do a plumbing takeoff](/guides/how-to-do-a-plumbing-takeoff) covers the manual method.

## Where takeoff software is the better choice

Software wins when these line up:

- **Steady volume.** If you price several jobs every week, the fixed costs spread across many takeoffs.
- **An estimator with time.** The tool does nothing without someone to run it. If that person is already full, software adds a task instead of removing one.
- **Repeat building types.** Houses from one builder, restaurant fit-outs, school additions: saved types and templates carry from job to job.
- **Control of the method.** You decide how risers are measured and which allowances apply, the plans stay in your office, and every job adds to your own records.

What software will not do is read the drawings for you or catch a missed riser.

## What AI takeoff tools do well, and where they need a person

AI takeoff tools are improving and worth testing. They do best at counting repeated symbols across many sheets (fixtures, sprinkler heads, devices) and at a first pass you can correct, which on clean, repeated layouts such as apartment units or a row of identical restrooms can save real time. They need a person where plumbing drawings are least explicit:

- **Single-line piping and its fittings.** A line on a plan is a route, not a pipe list: materials come from the specification, sizes change along the run, and one line type can stand for two or three materials. The fittings a single-line drawing leaves out come from an allowance built on history (see [pipe waste and fitting allowances](/guides/plumbing-pipe-waste-and-fitting-allowances)).
- **Vertical runs.** Plan views do not show how far pipe rises, and riser diagrams are schematic, so lengths come from floor-to-floor heights, not from scaling the diagram.
- **Specification items.** Hangers, insulation, firestopping, testing and disinfection sit in the specification, not in the symbols on the plan.
- **Project-specific symbols.** The legend decides what a symbol means, and a tool trained on common symbols can misread a project's own.
- **Conflicts and addenda.** When two sheets disagree, a tool may pick one without telling you; a person logs the conflict as an RFI.

### How to test an AI takeoff tool before you trust it

1. Pick a finished job with a full set and your final takeoff.
2. Run the tool with no help, and check that you can see and edit every item it detected.
3. Compare counts first, then pipe by size and material, then vertical runs.
4. Log every miss and every false item by system.
5. Time the review needed to bring the output up to your standard.
6. Repeat on a different building type before you rely on it.

<Callout type="tip" title="Judge the review time, not the run time">

A tool that finishes in minutes but needs hours of checking has not saved the hours. Hold a service to the same test: ask for a sample on a set like yours, not a demo set.

</Callout>

## Where a takeoff service is the better choice

A service earns its fee when hours, not skill, are the constraint.

- **Bid pile-ups.** Three bids due Friday and one estimator: send the overflow out and keep the jobs you want to measure.
- **Unfamiliar work.** Hospitals, laboratories, industrial plants and multi-story buildings bring specialty piping and risers on many floors, where a second estimator can catch a costly miss before it reaches your bid.
- **No estimator on staff.** An owner who estimates at night can hand off the measuring and keep the pricing.
- **A second set of numbers.** An independent takeoff on a large bid shows where your quantities and someone else's disagree, before the bid goes in.

A service has limits too:

- It depends on the provider's calendar, so ask how fast a quote and a delivery really are.
- It does not know your crews. You price the quantities, or order a priced estimate that follows the project ZIP code, including union, open-shop and prevailing-wage labor.
- Your plans leave your office. Ask for an NDA, and ask whether the provider also bids or builds. We do neither, so your plans and pricing stay yours.
- Revisions need a clear policy, so ask what an addendum costs before you send the set.

## How to compare the real cost of software, AI tools and a service

A subscription and a project fee only compare once you add the hours around them, and estimator time is usually the biggest in-house line. Time your last five takeoffs, first click to final check, and use that average instead of anyone's claim, including ours. ## Which option fits your situation

Most decisions turn on how many bids you price, how bunched they are, and who is free to check the work.

| Your situation | Likely fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Several similar jobs a week and an estimator with open time | Software | Fixed costs spread across many jobs, and templates carry over |
| One or two bids a month and no estimator | A service, or a manual takeoff on small jobs | A subscription would sit idle between bids |
| Bids bunched around bid dates | A hybrid | Measure what fits and send the overflow out |
| A building type you rarely bid | A service, or a second check | An unfamiliar set hides scope you do not know to look for |
| A general contractor comparing plumbing bids | A [plumbing quantity takeoff](/services/plumbing-takeoff#quantity-or-material-takeoff) from a service | Every bidder prices the same quantities |
| A budget before drawings exist | A [preliminary plumbing estimate](/services/preliminary-plumbing-estimate) | Nothing to take off yet: the budget comes from fixture counts, floor area and sketches |

A hybrid works on two conditions. Set the overflow rule before bid week (any set over a certain sheet count, or with a system you rarely price), because bid day is too late. And make files from different sources match: one workbook layout, one set of units and one naming scheme, with the provider filling your template where you have one.
