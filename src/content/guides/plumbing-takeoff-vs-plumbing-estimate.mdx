Plumbing takeoff vs estimate comes down to one split: a takeoff measures, an estimate prices. A plumbing takeoff lists what the drawings show (fixture counts, pipe lengths by size and material, fittings, valves and equipment), each line traced to a sheet. A plumbing estimate prices that list with labor, material, equipment, subcontracts, overhead and profit, and ends in a number you can bid. The takeoff comes first, and many plumbing subcontractors need only that part.

<KeyTakeaways>

- Accuracy is built in the takeoff. The estimate inherits every quantity, mistakes included.
- Order the takeoff when your own rates win your bids, and the estimate when you need a number.
- Compare bids on net quantities, not on purchase quantities with unstated allowances.

</KeyTakeaways>

## What a plumbing takeoff contains and what an estimate adds

A plumbing takeoff is a measured list with no prices. A complete one:

- is organized by system, so waste and vent, water, storm and gas each have their own section
- gives the size, material and unit on every line
- ties every quantity to a sheet and detail, so another estimator can check it
- states what it excludes and what it assumed where the drawings were silent

An estimate starts from those quantities and adds labor hours by system and phase, material priced to the project location, equipment, subcontracts, general conditions, overhead, profit and contingency. A good one writes down its inclusions, exclusions, assumptions and estimate class. Productivity, working conditions, risk and bid strategy are judgment calls a takeoff never makes, so two estimators can share the same quantities and still price them differently. The guides on [how to do a plumbing takeoff](/guides/how-to-do-a-plumbing-takeoff) and [how to estimate a plumbing job](/guides/how-to-estimate-a-plumbing-job) cover each method.

## Plumbing takeoff vs estimate: a side-by-side comparison

| Aspect | Plumbing takeoff | Plumbing estimate |
|---|---|---|
| Question it answers | What is on the drawings, and how much of it? | What will it cost to build? |
| What it contains | Counts, lengths and areas by size, material and system | The quantities, plus labor, material, equipment, subcontracts and markups |
| Prices | None | Yes, at local rates or your own |
| Main inputs | Drawings, schedules and specifications | The takeoff, supplier quotes, labor units, crew rates and bid documents |
| Typical user | A subcontractor pricing at its own rates, or a general contractor sending the same quantities to every bidder | Anyone who needs a number to bid, budget or negotiate |
| How accuracy is judged | Complete and traceable: every system measured, every line tied to a sheet, gaps logged, not guessed | A stated [accuracy range and estimate class](/services/plumbing-estimating), both set by how complete the design is |
| Where errors hide | Missed vertical runs, miscounted fixtures, missed addenda | Everything in the takeoff, plus labor assumptions, stale prices and markup math |

A riser missed in the takeoff is missed in material and again in labor, and no markup or contingency puts it back.

<Callout type="warn" title="Do not price from a takeoff you cannot trace">

A quantity with no sheet reference can be neither checked nor defended when an owner or general contractor asks where it came from. Whoever does the takeoff, ask for a sheet and detail reference on every line.

</Callout>

## Quantity takeoff vs material takeoff vs estimate

Contractors use three terms loosely, and the difference matters when you order:

- **Quantity takeoff (QTO):** net quantities as drawn, unpriced, with sheet references.
- **Material takeoff (MTO):** the same quantities in purchase units, with the allowance for cuts written out.
- **Estimate:** the quantities priced, labor included.

Here is one line carried through all three (the figures are illustrative):

| Document | What the line says | What you do with it |
|---|---|---|
| Quantity takeoff | 4 in. hubless cast iron waste, horizontal: 200 LF, sheet P-101 | Price it at your own rates, or give the same 200 LF to every bidder |
| Material takeoff | 200 LF net plus a 10 LF cut allowance: 210 LF, bought as 21 lengths of 10 ft, with couplings and fittings by size | Send it to a supplier for a quote or an order |
| Estimate | Material priced on the 210 LF bought, labor hours on the 200 LF installed, plus equipment and markup | Bid the price, or carry it in a budget |

Net quantities compare line by line between two estimators; purchase quantities compare only when the allowances are stated. So keep the net quantity as the source and apply [pipe waste and fitting allowances](/guides/plumbing-pipe-waste-and-fitting-allowances) in the material list, where they stay visible. ## Which to order: a takeoff, an estimate or both

- **You price with your own labor units and supplier quotes.** Order the takeoff and price it yourself. Your rates are what win bids, so someone else's pricing adds little.
- **You need a number to bid, budget or check.** Order a [plumbing estimate](/services/plumbing-estimating). A general contractor or owner checking a plumbing bid needs one to compare against; one who wants every plumbing bidder on equal quantities sends them all a quantity takeoff instead.
- **The drawings are not finished.** Order a [preliminary plumbing estimate](/services/preliminary-plumbing-estimate), built from fixture counts, floor area and sketches.
- **You are buying material.** Order the material takeoff, the list a supplier can quote.
- **You want your own number and a check number.** Order the estimate, price its quantities at your own rates, and compare the two totals system by system.
