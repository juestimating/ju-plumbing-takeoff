A plumbing waste factor is the allowance you add to net pipe quantities for material you buy but do not install: offcuts too short to use, miscuts, damaged pieces and handling losses. It exists because pipe is bought in stock lengths, not by the foot you need, so any run that is not a clean multiple leaves a remainder. A fitting allowance covers the fittings the drawings cannot show, and contingency, which covers risk, is a separate line again. Set both allowances from your own purchasing records and write them into the estimate so a reviewer can challenge them. Here, waste means offcuts and scrap, not sanitary waste piping.

## Net quantity, quantity with allowance and quantity to buy

Keep three quantities apart in the estimate. Merging them is how allowances become invisible.

| Quantity | What it is | What you use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Net quantity | Length or count exactly as drawn and measured | Comparing bids, checking against the drawings, and driving labor hours |
| Quantity with allowance | Net quantity plus the waste or fitting allowance | Material pricing and supplier quotes |
| Quantity to buy | The above, rounded up to stock lengths and pack sizes | Purchase orders |

Labor runs on the net quantity, because that is what the crew installs, and material runs on the quantity to buy. Bids should be compared on net quantities, since purchase quantities only compare when every allowance is stated. A [plumbing quantity takeoff](/services/plumbing-takeoff#quantity-or-material-takeoff) keeps the net numbers separate for that reason.

## Where pipe waste and missing fittings come from

- **Cutting from stock lengths.** An offcut is only worth something if another run needs that length.
- **Many short runs.** Short runs of one size waste more than a few long ones. Large, costly pipe justifies a cut list; small branch pipe is usually handled with an allowance.
- **Fitting length.** Pipe measured through a fitting's centerline is longer than the pipe you cut. Many estimators measure straight through and let the allowance cover the difference, so pick one method and apply it every time.
- **Handling and damage.** Cracked, dented or miscut pieces, material left out in the weather, and pieces cut out after a failed test.
- **Consumables.** Solvent cement and primer, solder and flux, thread sealant, gaskets and clamps are used per joint, not per foot, so carry them by joint or on their own line.
- **Fittings a single line cannot show.** Offsets, couplings, transitions and adapters that crews need and a single-line drawing does not draw.

## How to set a plumbing waste factor from your own history

1. **Split by material and size class.** Copper, PVC, cast iron and PEX behave differently, and small branch pipe is not large main pipe.
2. **Pull completed jobs.** From job-cost and purchasing records, take what you bought, less returns and stock carried to the next job. What is left is what you consumed.
3. **Remove scope that was not in the takeoff.** Change orders and added work buy material the original takeoff never counted.
4. **Divide consumed by net.** A ratio of 1.10, for example, means an allowance of 10 percent.
5. **Group by job type.** New construction with open framing and a tight remodel behave differently, so keep them apart.
6. **Record the factor with its source and date,** and refresh it as jobs close or when a material, supplier or crew changes.

Keep one closeout row per material and size class on every job:

| Column | How to get it |
|---|---|
| Job and type | New build, remodel or tenant improvement |
| Material and size class | For example, 2 in. copper tube |
| Net takeoff quantity | From the final takeoff, adjusted for change orders |
| Purchased | From invoices |
| Returned or carried over | Credits, and stock moved to the next job |
| Consumed | Purchased, less returned or carried over |
| Ratio | Consumed divided by net |

If you have no history yet, start with a stated, provisional figure and say so in the basis. Ask your foreman and purchaser which size always runs short and which material gets damaged on site. Do not adopt an industry average without knowing which building types and methods stand behind it, and start collecting records on the next job.

## Fitting allowances: isometrics versus single-line drawings

| What the drawings show | How to count fittings | What the allowance covers |
|---|---|---|
| A coordinated model or shop drawings with every fitting | Count each fitting by type and size as drawn | Field changes and parts at stock-length joints, usually small |
| Isometrics for the system | Count from the isometric, system by system | Items the isometric leaves out, such as couplings and adapters |
| Single-line plans with riser diagrams | Count the direction changes, tees, wyes and branch connections you can see, and follow each riser floor by floor | Offsets and fittings a single line cannot show, set from your history |
| Conceptual or schematic drawings | Estimate from fixture counts and floor area | Everything not drawn, stated as an assumption |

Three rules keep fitting allowances honest. Count what is drawn first, and add the allowance only for what is not. Set it by system, size and material, because one number for all fittings pads the cheap ones and shorts the expensive ones. And express it in a form you can track, such as fittings per 100 LF of each size and material, then measure that ratio on completed jobs.

An isometric shrinks the fitting allowance but does not remove the waste factor, because pipe is still cut from stock lengths. Never add a percentage on top of a fitting count taken from an isometric, which counts the same fittings twice. The guide on [how to do a plumbing takeoff](/guides/how-to-do-a-plumbing-takeoff) shows where fitting counts sit in the measuring order.

## A worked example with illustrative numbers

The numbers below are arbitrary round figures chosen to show the arithmetic. They are not recommended allowances and they are not industry averages.

| Item | Net from drawings | Allowance (example) | With allowance | To buy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 in. copper tube | 200 LF | 10 percent | 220 LF | 11 lengths of 20 ft |
| 4 in. hubless cast iron pipe | 150 LF | 5 percent | 157.5 LF | 16 lengths of 10 ft |
| 2 in. elbows counted from the plan | 24 EA | 10 percent | 26.4 EA | 27 EA |
| 2 in. tees counted from the plan | 10 EA | 10 percent | 11 EA | 11 EA |
| 4 in. couplings counted from the isometric | 40 EA | none, every joint counted | 40 EA | 40 EA |

The stock lengths are assumed, so check them with your supplier. Each pipe line multiplies the net length by the factor and rounds up to whole lengths: 157.5 LF is 15.75 lengths of 10 ft, so you buy 16. Round the total for each size and material this way, not each run, unless crews truly cannot reuse offcuts. Labor still runs on the 150 LF and 200 LF of net pipe.

Run lengths matter as much as the percentage. Take 2 in. pipe cut from 20 ft lengths. Ten runs of 12 ft need 10 lengths if each 8 ft remainder is scrapped: 200 LF bought for 120 LF installed, or about two-thirds extra. Add ten runs of 8 ft and each length yields one 12 ft piece and one 8 ft piece, so the same 10 lengths cover all 20 runs, 200 LF in total, with nothing left over (ignoring saw cuts and fitting length).

A basis note for this example might read:

> Pipe quantities include a cut allowance of 10 percent on copper and 5 percent on cast iron, rounded up to whole stock lengths. Fittings counted from the plans carry a further 10 percent for fittings the single-line drawings do not show; couplings counted from the isometric carry none. Labor is based on net quantities.

## Document allowances so the estimate can be checked

Give net quantity, allowance, quantity with allowance and quantity to buy their own columns, so the allowance can be seen and changed. Keep each factor in one named place with a note on what it covers, where it came from and whether it is still provisional.
