How to do a plumbing takeoff the way a working estimator does it: prepare the plan set, count the fixtures, measure pipe one system at a time, add fittings, valves, hangers and insulation, take off equipment and underground work, then reconcile the totals against the drawings. Each step feeds the next: the fixture count sets up the waste, vent and water piping, and pipe lengths drive the fittings and hangers that go with them.

A takeoff measures. It does not price. You finish with counts, lengths and areas, each tied to the sheet it came from, and pricing them is the separate step that turns a [plumbing takeoff into an estimate](/guides/plumbing-takeoff-vs-plumbing-estimate).

## The 10 steps of a plumbing takeoff

| Step | What you do | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Prepare the set | A complete, calibrated set and a list of questions |
| 2 | Count the fixtures | The fixture list and the connections each one needs |
| 3 | Measure waste and vent | Pipe by size and material, fittings, drains, cleanouts |
| 4 | Measure water, storm and gas | Pipe by size and material, valves, insulation |
| 5 | Follow the risers | Vertical lengths and the items repeated on each floor |
| 6 | Add fittings, valves, hangers and insulation | The items tied to each run |
| 7 | Take off equipment | Units, connections, pads and trade boundaries |
| 8 | Take off underground work | Trench lengths by depth, pipe, bedding and backfill |
| 9 | Reconcile | A checked list and a scope-gap log |
| 10 | Export | A workbook by system and the marked-up plans |

## Prepare the plan set first

Check the sheet index against the sheets in your hands, because a missing sheet is a missing system. Then read the legend, general notes and Division 22 specification before you measure: the specification assigns pipe materials by service and location, so one line type can hide two or three materials. The guide on [how to read plumbing plans](/guides/how-to-read-plumbing-plans) explains what each sheet, symbol and schedule tells you.

**Calibrate every sheet.** Do not trust the title block: a set resized for printing or exported at another page size carries the wrong scale. Calibrate each sheet against a printed dimension or a grid-line spacing, confirm it with a second dimension, and flag sheets drawn at more than one scale or marked not to scale. Details usually carry their own scale.

**Decide how you will record each line.** Every line answers the same questions: what it is, what size and material, how many, in what unit, and where it came from. Break the takeoff down by system and then by floor, area or phase before you start. You can total quantities later, but you cannot split them later without measuring again. A line looks like this (the quantities are illustrative):

| Description | Size | Material | Qty | Unit | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Waste, horizontal main | 4 in. | Hubless cast iron | 200 | LF | Sheet P-101, detail on P-501 |
| Water closet WC-1 | n/a | Floor mounted | 10 | EA | Schedule on P-001, plan P-101 |
| Cold water, branch | 3/4 in. | Copper | 150 | LF | Sheet P-102 |

Set up one takeoff type per system, material and size, such as 4 in. hubless cast iron waste or 3/4 in. copper cold water, and one color per system, so a second estimator and the foreman can read the marked-up plans.

**Separate new work from existing work on remodels.** Measure existing piping to remain, piping to be removed and new piping as separate types, and give demolition, capping and each tie-in its own line. The demolition plans hold that scope.

## Count the plumbing fixtures first, from the schedule

Most fixtures set a waste, a vent and one or two supplies, so the fixture list drives the piping. Count from the schedule, then confirm each count on the plans.

1. **List the schedule.** Write down every tag with its description, connection sizes and remarks.
2. **Count on the plans.** Go room by room and floor by floor, and mark each symbol as you count it so nothing is counted twice.
3. **Reconcile both ways.** A tag on the schedule that never appears on a plan, or a symbol with no schedule entry, goes on your gap list.
4. **Attach what travels with the fixture.** Carriers and supports, flush valves, faucets, stops and supplies, traps, trap primers, protective covers on accessible fixtures and mixing valves each need a line, with a note on who furnishes it. Owner-furnished fixtures still need rough-in and setting labor.
5. **Multiply repeated layouts last.** Count a typical apartment, hotel room or classroom once and multiply, but only after you confirm the units match. Mirrored, accessible and end units often differ.

If the architectural and plumbing plans show different fixture counts, do not choose one silently. Carry the count you can defend from the plumbing sheets, log the conflict and send it as an RFI before bid day.

## Measure the piping one system at a time

Measure each system on its own, by size and material, along the drawn route rather than a straight line, and mark each run as you go. The [plumbing takeoff checklist](/guides/plumbing-takeoff-checklist) lists every item in each system.

| System | Where to start | The item most often missed |
|---|---|---|
| Sanitary waste and vent | Each fixture group, traced to its stack or main, with above-slab and below-slab piping as separate types | The vertical drop at each fixture, vents carried to the roof with each roof penetration, and a coupling at every hubless joint |
| Domestic water | The service entrance (meter, backflow preventer, pressure-reducing valve, main shutoff), then cold, hot and return as separate runs | Size changes: each size is its own run, because averaging hides the large pipe, which costs the most per foot |
| Storm drainage | Roof drains and overflow drains on the roof plan, then each leader to the building exit | The building-to-site boundary, where the civil sheets take over, and overflow piping that runs separately |
| Gas piping | The gas plan and riser diagram, from the meter or regulator to each appliance | Connections drawn only on the mechanical or kitchen sheets. Take sizes as drawn, because sizing is design work |
| Medical gas and fire sprinkler | The bid instructions, to confirm who carries them | The scope itself, since a separate trade often carries both. Sprinkler heads come from the reflected ceiling plan |

Give grease, acid and indirect waste their own takeoff types, because their materials and joints can differ.

## Follow every riser floor to floor to catch vertical runs

Plan views show where pipe runs across a floor, not how far it rises, so vertical piping is one of the easiest quantities to miss.

- **Find the riser diagram.** It shows each stack with floors, sizes, valves, cleanouts and connected fixtures. It is usually schematic, so do not scale it.
- **Take heights from the architecture.** Floor-to-floor heights come from the architectural and wall sections; add the extra length for offsets and connections on each floor.
- **Match the diagram to the plans.** Every riser should appear on each floor plan it serves, and every stack on a plan should have a riser entry. Mismatches go on the gap list.
- **Follow each riser to both ends:** down to the underground connection, and up to the roof for vents and roof drain leaders.
- **Count what repeats on every floor.** Valves, cleanouts, offsets, riser clamps and firestopping sit at each level.
- **Measure by size band.** Water and gas risers usually step down as they rise, and any stack can change size where the diagram shows it, so measure each size band rather than one floor multiplied.

For example, a riser that serves 5 floors at an illustrative 12 ft floor to floor is 60 ft of pipe before offsets, the run to the roof and the connection below the slab. Use the heights in your own drawings.

## Add fittings, valves, hangers and insulation

These items carry a large share of the labor and are the first to be skipped. Count them as you measure each run, because a separate pass at the end misses runs and loses the sheet reference.

| Item | Measured in | Taken from |
|---|---|---|
| Pipe | LF, by size and material | Plans, risers and details |
| Fittings | EA, by type, size and material | Isometrics, or plan counts plus a documented allowance |
| Valves | EA, by type and size | Plans, riser diagrams and specification |
| Hangers and supports | EA, by pipe size and type | Specification and support details |
| Insulation | LF by pipe size and thickness, EA for fittings | Specification |
| Fixtures and trim | EA, by tag | Fixture schedule |
| Equipment | EA, by tag | Equipment schedule |
| Drains and cleanouts | EA, by type and size | Plans and details |
| Testing and disinfection | LS | Specification |
| Trench and backfill | LF or CY, by depth band | Civil and underground plans |

**Fittings.** Count from isometrics where they exist. On single-line drawings, count the direction changes, tees and branches you can see and carry an allowance for the rest on its own line, apart from the net count. The guide to [pipe waste and fitting allowances](/guides/plumbing-pipe-waste-and-fitting-allowances) explains how to set one.

**Valves.** Count the symbols, then read the specification, which can call for valves the drawings omit, such as isolation valves at every branch.

**Hangers and supports.** Take the spacing by pipe size and material from the specification or the governing code, not from memory, divide each run by it, then add the supports required at fittings, risers and equipment.

**Insulation and accessories.** Each service, pipe size and thickness is its own insulation type, with jackets, fitting covers and vapor barrier on cold lines where specified. Sleeves, escutcheons, pipe identification, seismic bracing where required and access panels each need a note on who furnishes and who installs.

## Equipment and underground: where the scope stops

Take each unit on the equipment schedule with its connections, valves and accessories, and write down which trade owns each connection, flue, condensate drain and pad, because the plumbing and mechanical boundary is where work most often falls between trades.

Underground piping is its own takeoff, because trenching, bedding, backfill and compaction sit on top of the pipe. Read the inverts from the civil profile, group trench length by depth band, and state the excavation split: if the site contractor digs and backfills, say so, and if you do, price it.

## Reconcile the takeoff before you price it

1. **Fixtures against connections.** Every fixture has its waste, vent and supplies. A missing connection means a missing run.
2. **Risers against floors.** Each riser appears at every floor it serves, from the underground connection to the roof.
3. **Lengths against a known dimension.** Spot-check two or three runs against a dimension string or a room width.
4. **Specification against takeoff.** Every Division 22 section with work in it has a takeoff line, or an exclusion in the basis.
5. **Addenda against the takeoff.** Each revised sheet was re-measured, not just relabeled.
6. **Marked-up plans against the drawings.** Piping, fixtures or equipment with no markup is a candidate for a miss.
7. **Totals against your history.** Compare pipe per fixture or per floor area with similar jobs. A large gap either way deserves a second look.
8. **A second estimator.** Someone who did not measure the set reads the marked-up plans and the summary. Every file we deliver gets this check.

Write down what you included, excluded and assumed where the drawings were silent.
