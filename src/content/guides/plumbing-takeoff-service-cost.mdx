## How plumbing takeoff services price their work

Five pricing models cover most of what you will be quoted. Each has a weak point, and a question that exposes it.

### Fixed price per project

The provider reviews the set and quotes one price for a stated deliverable, so you know the cost before work starts. The weak point is scope: one quote includes addenda and another bills them, one covers underground work and another leaves it out, so ask what each one includes before you compare totals. This is how we price.

### Price per sheet

Easy to calculate, and blunt: a sheet of details and a floor plan with every run to trace are not the same work, and a set can carry a lot of plumbing on a few sheets. Ask what counts as a sheet, whether repeated floors count separately, and whether schedules and details cost the same as plans.

### Hourly

You pay for time, which suits open-ended work such as checking someone else's takeoff and fits badly with a bid date. The bill is unknown until the work is done, and two hourly rates say little about the hours each provider will use. Ask for an estimate of hours and a cap.

### Monthly package or dedicated estimator

A flat monthly fee for a set number of projects gives a predictable cost when you send work most weeks, and goes to waste when you do not, so add up a typical month at per-project prices and compare it with the fee. A dedicated estimator for a full bid calendar is a custom quote, best compared with the full cost of a payroll position rather than with per-project prices. The [monthly plumbing estimating](/services/monthly-plumbing-estimating) page covers both.

### A takeoff from your supplier

Some suppliers offer takeoffs built around their own products, sometimes at no charge. Ask whether each line names its sheet, whether the file can be edited and re-priced, and whether it goes past material to hangers, insulation, penetrations and equipment connections. It may also tie the quantities to one catalog. An independent takeoff costs money, but its quantities work with any supplier, and a supplier's list makes a useful second opinion beside it.

## How to compare plumbing takeoff quotes line by line

Put every quote into the same grid before you look at the totals.

| What to ask | Why it matters | A good answer |
|---|---|---|
| What exactly is delivered? | Quantities, a material list and a priced estimate are different documents | A named list: workbook, PDF summary, marked-up plans, basis of estimate, scope-gap log |
| Is the file editable? | You will re-price it and check it | An Excel workbook with live formulas and no locks |
| Does each line cite a sheet? | A number with no sheet behind it cannot be checked | A sheet and detail reference on every quantity |
| Which systems are included? | Underground, gas, medical gas, fire sprinkler and equipment are often quoted separately | A system list and a list of exclusions |
| How are addenda handled? | Addenda arrive in the middle of the bid | Revisions included, scope changes quoted separately |
| Who checks the work? | One reader misses things | A second estimator on every file |
| What is the turnaround, in writing? | Bid dates do not move | A stated window, such as 24–72 hours, and a delivery date in the quote |
| What happens when drawings conflict? | A guess hides risk | A scope-gap log you can send as RFIs |
| Is the price fixed? | Open-ended pricing hides the total | A fixed quote before work starts |
| Is there an NDA? | Plans and pricing are sensitive | NDA on request, and no sharing between clients |

Compare the same scope in the same unit: if one quote leaves out scope the others carry, add it to that quote's price first. A takeoff price buys quantities, not a bid number. Labor, material pricing and markups come with an estimate, and pipe sizing, design, code review and hydraulic calculations sit outside any takeoff. [Plumbing takeoff vs plumbing estimate](/guides/plumbing-takeoff-vs-plumbing-estimate) shows what each document contains, so you can tell whether two quotes are for the same thing.

Three more warning signs do not fit the grid:

- **Promises of perfect accuracy.** Honest providers state a range and the assumptions behind it.
- **A final price before anyone has opened the set.** That is a guess. A fixed quote should follow a review of your plans.
- **Fees tied to the project's dollar value.** The effort follows the drawings, not the contract.

## The cost of doing a takeoff in-house, as a formula

Use your own records, not salary surveys or market averages, and start with five numbers:

- **H:** estimator hours per takeoff, from opening the set to the final check, including interruptions and addenda. Time your last five takeoffs and average them.
- **R:** that person's loaded hourly cost, which is total annual cost (wages, burden and your overhead share) divided by the hours actually available for estimating.
- **V:** review and rework per takeoff, in cost terms: the second reader's time, plus re-measuring and fixing errors found late.
- **F:** the fixed monthly cost of software, training and hardware.
- **N:** takeoffs you complete per month.

The in-house cost of one takeoff is (H × R) + V + (F ÷ N). The cost of sending the same takeoff out is the fee plus B × R, where B is the time you spend briefing the provider and reviewing the file.

If H × R + V is already higher than the cost of sending the work out, a service is cheaper at every volume. If it is lower, software wins once N is large enough to spread F. The break-even is N = F ÷ (cost of sending out, less H × R, less V), and if the figure in brackets is zero or negative there is no break-even. Run it for a slow month, a normal month and a peak month, because the answer changes with N.

Two costs sit outside the formula. Capacity is the value of the bids you could not submit because nobody had the hours, and your win history puts a number on it. Risk is what a missed riser costs, the same whoever missed it, so ask how each option checks its work. ## How to keep a plumbing takeoff price down without cutting corners

Price follows effort, so cut the effort on our side without cutting the checking.

- **Send the complete set once.** Plumbing, architectural and civil sheets, the specification and every addendum so far. Partial sets and drip-fed addenda create rework.
- **Say which systems are yours.** If gas, medical gas or fire sprinkler belong to another trade, tell us: fewer systems to measure means a smaller quote.
- **Order the right deliverable.** If you price at your own rates, a takeoff is enough; the [sample files](/samples) show what a takeoff and an estimate each contain.
- **Give a realistic bid date.** Rush moves the price, and a date that leaves room for 24–72 hours does not.
- **Say what is already decided.** If an excavation, insulation or design-build sprinkler contractor already carries part of the work, that section comes off the list and the basis of estimate records it as an exclusion.
