Plumbing estimating mistakes sort into four kinds: quantity misses (risers, fixtures, addenda), scope misses (insulation, firestopping, coring, testing, excavation), pricing mistakes (labor, stale quotes, general conditions) and markup math. This guide covers 12 of them, each with a symptom you can spot in your own estimate and a fix you can apply before you submit. Most are omissions, not math errors, and many are work that no drawing shows as a symbol. Some lose the bid by pricing too high. The worse ones win it by pricing too low.

| 3 | Missed addenda | Quantities do not match the latest sheets | Log every addendum, re-measure revised sheets and list them on the bid form |

## Quantity mistakes: risers, fixtures and addenda

### 1. Missed risers and vertical runs

A plan view shows the route across a floor, not the climb between floors. Take floor-to-floor heights from the architectural sections, and count valves, cleanouts, clamps and firestops at every floor, not only the first. The guide on [how to do a plumbing takeoff](/guides/how-to-do-a-plumbing-takeoff) shows the sequence.

### 2. Double-counted or uncounted fixtures

The same fixture can appear on the architectural, enlarged restroom and plumbing plans, and a typical-unit note can hide many more. Mirrored, accessible and end units often differ, and kitchen and equipment sheets add supplies and indirect waste that the plumbing sheets may not show.

### 3. Missed addenda

A set that looks unchanged can carry a new riser diagram, and a reply to another bidder's question can add work even when no sheet changes.

## Scope mistakes: required work that is not drawn

### 4. Insulation left out

The specification, not the plans, says what is insulated and how thick. Three items are easy to miss: horizontal storm piping and roof drain bodies, the hot water return, and the protective covers under accessible lavatories.

### 5. Firestopping and sleeves skipped

Rated assemblies appear on the architectural life-safety and wall-type sheets, not always on the plumbing sheets. If the specification gives firestopping to another trade, record "by others" in writing.

### 6. Core drilling, cutting and patching ignored

Check the structural notes before you price: post-tensioned or heavily reinforced slabs may need scanning and approval before any coring, and that adds time and cost.

### 7. Testing, flushing and disinfection not carried

No one draws a test. Carry pressure and leak tests for waste, vent, storm and water piping, flushing and disinfection of the potable system, backflow assembly tests, gas pressure tests and camera inspection where the specification asks, plus sampling and laboratory fees. On medical gas, third-party verification is a separate scope, so confirm whether it is yours.

### 8. Excavation scope unclear

Settle in writing who digs, shores, dewaters, backfills, compacts, hauls spoil and restores the surface, then price trench by depth band from the invert elevations, not from a flat allowance per foot.

## Pricing mistakes: labor, prices and general conditions

### 9. Labor that ignores height and phasing

A labor unit from open, ground-level work is wrong for a high lobby ceiling or the crowded space above a hospital corridor ceiling. Count mobilizations on phased work, because every return carries setup and cleanup, and carry hidden conditions on remodels as a stated allowance or a clear exclusion instead of hoping.

### 10. Stale prices and no escalation

A bid on a job that buys out months later can outlive its quotes, and large pipe, specialty valves, pumps and water heaters can carry long lead times. Check each quote's validity against the likely buyout date, and state how long your price holds.

### 11. Permits and general conditions left out

Price permits, bonds, supervision, mobilization, submittals and closeout for the duration of the job, not only the labor hours, from the general conditions list in [how to estimate a plumbing job](/guides/how-to-estimate-a-plumbing-job).

## Markup mistakes: markup is not margin

### 12. Markup math that earns less than you planned

Mixing markup on cost with margin on price quietly lowers your profit on every job, and the guide on [how to estimate a plumbing job](/guides/how-to-estimate-a-plumbing-job) works through an example. The second trap is the base: overhead and profit applied to labor only, to material only, or twice to a subcontract. A quick test: subtract your direct cost from the final price, divide by the price, and compare the result with the overhead and profit share you meant to earn. If they differ, the math is off.

## Build a mistake log so each miss happens only once

Every estimator misses something, and the ones who improve write it down. After each bid, and again after each job closes, record what was missed, what changed after award and what an addendum or RFI revealed. Sort the entries by system and by cause (quantity, scope, price, labor or markup), and feed the list back into your checklist and your labor units.

When bid results are published, compare them with yours. Where a tabulation shows unit prices, alternates or line items, a large gap usually means one side omitted work or priced a different scope. Treat each gap as a question to answer, not a verdict on the estimate.
