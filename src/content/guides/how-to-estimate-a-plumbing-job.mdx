How to estimate a plumbing job starts with scope, not numbers. Review the bid documents, take off the quantities, price material from supplier quotes, build labor from unit rates and crew costs, add equipment, subcontracts and general conditions, then overhead, profit and contingency, and check the total before it goes out. The takeoff supplies the quantities, and everything after it turns them into a price you can bid and build to. Rates, productivity and markups are left to you, because they come from your own history and your own market.

## What goes into a plumbing estimate

A plumbing estimate prices the plumbing scope from a takeoff of the drawings and ends in a number you can bid, budget or negotiate. Every estimate uses the same seven cost categories:

| Cost category | What goes in it | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Pipe, fittings, valves, hangers, insulation, fixtures and equipment | Takeoff quantities and supplier quotes |
| Labor | Installers, foremen, apprentices and burden | Quantities, your labor units and crew rates |
| Equipment | Lifts, excavators, rigging, threading and test equipment | Duration and rental or ownership cost |
| Subcontracts | Excavation, core drilling, firestopping, insulation, testing | Written subcontractor quotes |
| General conditions | Supervision, permits, bonds, insurance, mobilization, closeout | Duration and contract requirements |
| Overhead and profit | Office costs and the margin you carry | Your company numbers and bid strategy |
| Contingency and escalation | Risk and price movement before buyout | Your read of the drawings, market and schedule |

The takeoff supplies the quantities behind the first two lines, and the guide on [plumbing takeoff vs estimate](/guides/plumbing-takeoff-vs-plumbing-estimate) draws the line between the two.

## How to estimate a plumbing job: scope review and takeoff

### Review the bid documents first

Before you measure anything, decide what you are pricing:

- **Bid instructions:** the bid form, required alternates and unit prices, and bond and insurance requirements.
- **Contract type:** lump sum, unit price, cost-plus or design-build, which changes who carries risk.
- **Specifications:** Division 22 for materials, methods, testing, submittals, warranty and closeout, and Division 01 for phasing, working hours and cleanup.
- **Drawings and addenda:** the full set, every addendum logged by number and date.
- **Other trades:** who excavates, cores, firestops, furnishes pads, makes electrical connections and carries fire sprinkler and medical gas.
- **Existing conditions:** on a remodel, visit the site for access, hidden conditions and the material route.

Write the inclusions and exclusions as you read. Send each conflict or gap as a question with its sheet and detail reference, through the channel the instructions name, and record the answer, or the assumption you priced if none comes.

### Take off the quantities by system

A priced estimate inherits every error in the takeoff: a missed riser is missing from the material and the labor. The guide on [how to do a plumbing takeoff](/guides/how-to-do-a-plumbing-takeoff) covers the method. Break quantities by floor, area or phase as you measure, so labor can be adjusted without measuring again, and keep them net: the allowance for cuts and fittings belongs in material pricing, where it stays visible.

## Price material from written supplier quotes

Material is the easiest line to price and the easiest to price stale. Group it, because each group is quoted differently:

- **Bulk material:** pipe, fittings, hangers, insulation and consumables, from the supplier's list with your multiplier, or a written quote on larger jobs.
- **Valves and specialties:** by type, size and the manufacturers the specification names.
- **Fixtures and trim:** by the model on the schedule.
- **Equipment:** by model, with accessories, delivery and lead time. This group moves the total most, so get more than one quote.

Price the specified manufacturer in the base bid. Before you offer an equal, read the substitution procedure, because late substitutions are often refused, and show the equal as a clarification or an alternate. Send every supplier the same list with sizes, materials and quantities, and ask each for the date, validity, freight, tax treatment, lead time, restocking terms and exclusions.

Then convert net quantities to purchase quantities: pipe comes in stock lengths, fittings are bought whole and cuts leave offcuts. The guide to [pipe waste and fitting allowances](/guides/plumbing-pipe-waste-and-fitting-allowances) explains how to set the allowance. Price to the project ZIP code, not your home market, and confirm the tax treatment, which depends on the state and on who owns the project.

## Build labor from units, crew rates and conditions

Labor depends most on your own crews, so the method is simple and the inputs are yours.

1. **Choose a labor unit for each takeoff line:** hours per foot of a pipe size and material, or per fixture set. Start from your own job history, and check any published unit-labor data against what your crews actually do.
2. **Multiply quantity by labor unit** for base hours. For the arithmetic only: 200 units at 0.5 hour each is 100 base hours.
3. **Adjust for conditions,** each on its own line, because a hidden factor cannot be checked.
4. **Convert hours to cost with a burdened crew rate:** base wage plus fringe benefits plus payroll burden such as taxes and insurance, blended across the foreman, journeymen and apprentices.
5. **Split labor by phase:** underground, rough-in, trim and testing or closeout, because crews, rates and conditions differ by phase.

The conditions that move labor most:

- working height: lifts, scaffolds or long ladder work
- congestion above ceilings, in shafts and in crawl spaces
- phased work, with more than one mobilization
- occupied buildings, restricted hours and infection control
- long material handling distances and limited elevator access
- existing conditions on remodels, where the drawings may not match the building

Union work follows the collective bargaining agreement for the trade and area, fringes included, and open-shop rates follow your own payroll. Public work under a wage determination, federal Davis-Bacon or a state equivalent, sets minimum rates and adds payroll reporting.

Assemblies bundle a repeated unit, such as a lavatory with its carrier, stops, supplies, trap and trim, under one material list and one labor unit. They are fast but hide detail, so build each from a job you tracked and recheck it when the specification changes.

## Add equipment, subcontracts and general conditions

### Equipment

Equipment is what the crew uses, not what gets installed: lifts, excavators and trenchers, rigging or crane time, threading, grooving and press tools, test pumps and inspection cameras. Owned equipment gets a charge-out rate, and rented equipment a quote with delivery and pickup.

### Subcontracts

Excavation, core drilling, firestopping, insulation, testing or disinfection, fire sprinkler, medical gas and controls are often sublet. Get each quote in writing with a scope letter, and compare scope, not only price: a low quote that leaves out backfill is not low. Carry each subcontractor's exclusions into your own.

### General conditions, permits, bonds and insurance

These are the job costs that do not attach to a quantity:

- supervision and project management for the length of the job
- permits, plan review and inspection fees
- bonds, priced by the surety from the contract value (a bid bond usually carries no premium)
- insurance, including owner-required coverage, without carrying twice what labor burden already covers
- mobilization, cleanup, small tools, consumables and vehicles
- submittals, coordination drawings, as-builts, O&M manuals, closeout and warranty

Many of these scale with time on site, so take the duration from your labor: total labor hours divided by crew size and hours per day gives working days. If the bid schedule is shorter, the estimate needs a larger crew or overtime, and it should show the cost.

## Apply overhead, profit, contingency and escalation

- **Overhead** covers company costs that no single job pays for: office, estimating, accounting, vehicles and insurance not already in burden. Set the rate from last year's actual costs divided by your volume, and update it every year.
- **Profit** is a decision, not a formula. It reflects risk, competition and how much you want the job.
- **Contingency** holds money for risk in the documents: unclear details, incomplete design, a tight schedule. The less complete the drawings, the more you carry, and the more clearly you should say so.
- **Escalation** covers price movement between the estimate and purchase, which matters when buyout is months away or lead times are long.

Build the price up in the same written order on every bid, such as material tax, general conditions, overhead, profit, contingency and escalation, then the bond premium, which the surety charges on the final contract amount.

Markup is added to cost. Margin is the share of the price that is profit. A 25 percent markup on a direct cost of 800 gives a price of 1,000, but the 200 of profit is only 20 percent of that price. For a 25 percent margin, divide the cost by 0.75 and price the job at about 1,067. Either method works if you use it the same way on every bid, and the guide to [plumbing estimating mistakes](/guides/plumbing-estimating-mistakes) covers the other trap, markups on the wrong base.

## Add alternates, unit prices and allowances

Price each alternate as its own add or deduct, with its own labor, material and markup. Derive unit prices from the estimate so change orders match your base price, and carry allowances at the stated amount. The [plumbing bid checklist](/guides/plumbing-bid-checklist) covers how they go on the bid form.

## Review the estimate and state its class

[AACE International](https://web.aacei.org/) classifies estimates by how much of the project is defined, from Class 5, a concept-stage estimate built from little more than a program and an area, to Class 1, which rests on the most complete documents. The class describes the design behind the estimate, not the estimator's care, and the range of likely outcomes narrows as the class number drops. Our typical variance is ±5–10% on construction-document estimates and ±15–30% on conceptual budgets. Those are our figures, not AACE ranges.

### Write the basis of estimate

State what the number covers: the drawings and addenda used, the estimate class, inclusions and exclusions, assumptions where the drawings were silent, rates and price dates, markups, and alternates and unit prices. It settles arguments later, and a reviewer reads it first.

### Check it before it goes out

1. **Quantities.** Reconcile fixtures, risers and specification sections against the takeoff.
2. **Math.** Check every extension and sum. When every total is a formula, each number traces back to its takeoff line, as the [plumbing estimating spreadsheet](/guides/plumbing-estimating-spreadsheet) guide sets out.
3. **Reasonableness.** Compare totals with your own history: cost per fixture, cost per floor area, and labor against material by system. A large gap needs an explanation.
4. **Scope.** Read the specification once more against the estimate, confirm each subcontract covers its gaps, and check the addenda list.
5. **Markups.** The same base and order as every other bid.
6. **Second reader.** Someone who did not build the estimate reads the summary and the basis of estimate.

Then check the bid form with the [plumbing bid checklist](/guides/plumbing-bid-checklist). Build the estimate yourself when you know the building type, your numbers are current and the bid date leaves time for this review. Hand it off when volume outruns your hours: several bids in one week, a set outside your usual work, or a second opinion on a number you are about to commit to.
